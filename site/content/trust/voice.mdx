# Voice and shape

One command prints everything this page describes, and it is the source of truth:

```bash
slopweaver slack voice-rules
```

It prints **two layers**, and they are enforced differently. The first is a **shape** — how a reply should
read — and nothing refuses on any of it. The second is a **vocabulary** of exact rules, and a send refuses
authored prose matching one of them before any network call. On **every** outbound surface: a rule binds
the bot's own name as well as the owner's unless it says otherwise, and that default is where the leak was.

The split is deliberate. The vocabulary is a vocabulary, and a composer given only a banned-word list writes
off-voice prose that passes every gate. So the shape is briefed too, from the owner's own file:
`profile.json`'s `slackMe.voice.shapeMemoryFile` points at it, which is why an edit there changes what every
composing session reads.

<Callout type="warning">
  **The command's body wins over this page.** Its § "What the owner picked, example by example" carries the owner's own
  reactions to twenty real outbound examples, and those picks are more specific than any general guidance, including
  everything written below.
</Callout>

## The shape

<VoiceShape />

### The ceiling governs commentary, never the deliverable

**50 words is a ceiling, not a target.** An everyday reply defaults to one short line, and a wall of prose
past the ceiling is where the reader tunes out.

The counterbalance matters more than the ceiling, though, because it is the half that gets dropped: terseness
is not the same as cutting substance. Cutting too much is a measured failure, recurring seven times across the
transcript corpus, and "make it more concise" has stripped out the very information that gave a message its
value. The ceiling governs **commentary, recap, status and headlines**. It does not govern a plan, a spec, an
artifact the owner asked for, or an answer whose substance IS the deliverable. On a load-bearing document,
completeness wins.

### Lead with what, cut the how

The outcome people care about, not the pipeline that produced it: what was made, what it does, where it lives.
Then stop. A status update is "X is done, Y is next", not the phases you went through.

Progressive disclosure means the tight answer up front and the detail available if wanted. It does not mean
pre-empting every question with a wall nobody reads: say what happened, and leave room to ask why.

### One message to a channel, dripped replies in a thread

A **new top-level post to a public channel is ONE message.** Public posts may become discussion threads, so
keeping the ask in one top-level message gives the discussion one place to live and avoids splitting corrections
across messages. A follow-up correcting the first one reads as clutter, so get it right in the one message
instead.

Keep it short: the tag plus the PR link, then one sentence saying what it is. The evidence and the arguable half belong in the PR body, where a
reviewer already is.

**Inside an existing thread, or a DM, the owner drips.** A message carrying several claims goes out as a series of
short messages, each under the cap, the way a person actually types. A draft for a colleague reads as
fragments of a thought sent over four to six messages, never a single structured essay.

**In a persuasion thread, hold the ask.** When building a case for the team to adopt something, evidence goes
now and the ask waits for the reply. The held-back messages are ammunition for when someone bites. That is
the opposite instinct from a status report, where completeness up front is usually right.

### "I don't know if X" is a proposal, not a question

A common way of raising work, in about a tenth of every dictation, for example "I don't know if
it's worth checking the old tickets to see if ABC-1 had a script". The grammar hedges and the speech act
instructs. Do the thing, and report back if it turns out not to be worth it. Neither stall waiting for
confirmation nor treat it as a settled order. Same family as "I feel like we should", "I'd probably want to"
and "maybe worth".

### Hedging is a floor, not just a ceiling

The loudest measured trait, roughly one hedge every 55 words spoken, damping to about a fifth of that in
writing. The reason: a hedge is a correctness guard, since a hedged claim cannot be wrong. Stripping it changes
the claim rather than tightening it, and a claim-carrying message with **zero**
hedges is as off-voice as a stacked one. One light hedge per claim.

This is the one shape rule that is also an enforced rule, because it is the one the other three rule kinds
cannot express: `at-least-one` fires when none of a set appears. See below.

### The owner's actual voice

Lowercase, terse, casual, contractions, leads with the outcome, no corporate hedging, light emoji, swears
casually. "nice!", "pls", "cheers", "keep going down the backlog now", "yep, all green". Openers are `yo` or
`hey`, never `heya`. Praise is one word, then the real point as its own message. Walk-backs open on "actually"
or "wait" and never announce themselves. "shit" over "fuck" when swearing.

## What the owner picked, example by example

Twenty real outbound examples, each shown as it stood against three alternatives, and the owner reacted to every
one. These picks are turned into rules, and they beat everything above where they disagree.

- **The shape comes from the team, the sentences come from the owner.** On the PR examples the owner took the team's
  converging shape (headings, an acceptance-criteria block, a Test evidence table that media goes inside) and
  a different card's voice, the one that reads most like the owner talking. Never pick one and write the other's
  prose.
- **Risk and blast radius sit ABOVE "what does this PR do and why."** They are the more useful things to look at
  and should be the most prominent, because risk is what a reader is already trying to infer off the diff. A
  risk with no check behind it SAYS so (for example, that it has not been properly backtested and may be noisy)
  or lands with telemetry and the monitor that alerts on it, or carries a Datadog query standing in for a
  backtest. Never a risk line asserting "low" with nothing under it.
- **Lead with the one number that sells it**, in plain sentences, no section headers. The voice picked
  twice opens on the number ("N% of staging runs hit this one wait") and runs as prose.
- **Tag the people a message is about.** A post that names a person or a team names them with a tag, never in
  prose.
- **Never assign work to people who did not ask for it.** Telling four people to go and do something can rub
  people the wrong way. Owners are RECOMMENDATIONS and they go below the fold. The owner is tagged; they are not.
- **A Linear ticket carries the full shape, not the terse one** — problem, change, why this approach,
  acceptance criteria, in the owner's plain voice. The terse variant is too terse; the fuller one wins.
- **Never make the owner do arithmetic.** "N staff in scope, M of whom have answered nothing" forces a
  subtraction to find the number to chase, and leaves open whether there is any wiggle room. State the number to
  chase, and say whether it is firm.
- **Anything with groups in it is dot points**, nested where a group has sub-items, the base group everyone
  inherits first. Three groups in one paragraph is hard to read.
- **No riddles.** "Your groups are the base" makes the reader parse it in their head. Name the action: say what
  needs to be done.
- **When they said more than one thing, quote the line you are answering, then answer it.** The owner opens about
  9% of their own messages this way and the bot never did, because it answers in context. The quote earns its
  place by picking which point you answer, so a reply to a single-point message opens on the answer. Quoting
  back is most useful when someone said multiple things and you want to make it clear which exact message you
  are responding to. This is guidance everywhere and a
  gate in one place: `gmail draft --reply-to` refuses a reply opening on a quote of the whole parent, where
  it can read the parent's own text. What it cannot read, such as an HTML-only parent's history, passes, and
  `src/outbound/voice/redundantOpeningQuote.ts`'s header lists each case.
- **Where a thread already holds the fact, link it** and say what it probably means, rather than offering to
  go and fetch it.
- **A batch ask is two lines, then the list** — what they are, what is true of all of them, one line per
  person with their own items tagged, the total at the end. Nobody should open ten PRs to find out what they
  have in common.
- **An ask goes to the channel that owns it, tagging the person — never a DM out of nowhere.** An access request
  goes to the channel that handles access, tagging whoever grants it; a policy change to the channel that owns
  policy; a finding to a public channel tagging its stakeholder. A DM only where the owner named that DM; a review ask on
  the owner's pull request is DMed to its reviewer. Either way it carries "no rush" when there is none.
- **A question does not sit at the bottom of a card.**
- **An ambiguous attribution line is a defect, not a style point.** A line that pairs a
  person's name with a deliverable leaves the reader unable to tell whether that person is writing it or waiting
  on it. Say which.
- **A recurring status card is colour-coded and shows progress visually** — on track, at risk or off track
  against the project, the way a Linear project update reads at a glance, with colour-coded next steps under
  it.
- **An alerts digest leads on whether the service is on fire, and how much against previous days** — then the
  errors still open, their blast radius, the customer-facing impact and the severity. The reason is scanning:
  a reader should see at once that the other channels are mostly P3s while the platform channel holds a P2.

## Per-surface shape

| Surface                    | What it looks like                                                                                                         |
| -------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| a new post to a channel    | **one** top-level message, everything else collapsed                                                                       |
| a reply inside a thread    | short replies, dripped — the one-message rule governs new posts, not thread replies                                        |
| a one-person ask           | the channel that owns it, tagging them; a DM only where the owner named one or for a review ask; "no rush" when none       |
| a batch ask                | two lines, then the list: what they are, what is true of all of them, one line per person, the total at the end            |
| a pull-request description | the team's converging form; **risk and blast radius above** "what does this PR do"                                         |
| a Linear ticket            | the full shape — problem, change, why this approach, acceptance criteria                                                   |
| a recurring status card    | colour-coded, progress shown visually: on track / at risk / off track                                                      |
| an alerts digest           | is the service on fire, and how much against previous days — then the open errors, blast radius, customer impact, severity |

**Formatting that is not negotiable**, on any of them: no em-dashes in anything written as the owner (break into two
sentences, or use a comma); a blank line between paragraphs or bullet groups; URLs clickable and never wrapped
in backticks; a PR mention rendered as one hyperlink rather than a bare `#NNNN`; and no section-headered
structure in a personal message, because that reads as AI to a human even when the em-dashes are scrubbed.

Detail goes below an inline `Details` divider, in the PR diff, in an inline self-review comment, or in the
commit body. Never as a wall in the top-line reply.

## The vocabulary, and where it fires

<VoiceDoor />

The rules are exact by construction. `src/outbound/voice/exactRules.ts` can prove only a literal or a lexical
match, so cadence, complexity and rhetorical pattern stay in the voice prompt and never become a rule here.

| Kind           | It fires when                                          | Matching                        |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------- |
| `literal`      | the text appears anywhere in authored prose            | case-**sensitive**, no boundary |
| `word`         | the word appears with a lexical boundary either side   | case-insensitive                |
| `phrase`       | the phrase appears with a lexical boundary either side | case-insensitive                |
| `at-least-one` | **none** of a set appears — the hedge floor            | case-insensitive                |

Every kind takes an optional `actors` field naming which lane it binds, `me` or `bot`. **Absent means BOTH**,
and that default is load-bearing rather than a convenience: the measured leak was about one em-dash per hundred
sent messages, nearly all of them agent-authored posts going out under the owner's own account, so a rule with no `actors`
field must not read as "as-me only".

**What the rules never see.** `authoredProseOf` cuts a fenced block, a line opening with `>`, a URL, a Slack
link, inline code and a technical identifier out first, and an identifier leaves its surrounding punctuation
behind so that removing it never joins two sentences into one a rule could then match. What is left is prose
the owner would have typed, which is the only thing a voice rule has any business judging.

**One deliberate exception** re-runs after `slopweaver hatch open voice-rules --reason "<why>"`. The rule stays in force otherwise,
and a match refuses rather than rewrites: a refusal names the field, the line and the rule that fired, and
hands the sentence back.

## Why the rule list is not written down here

The rule SHAPE is public and lives in the repository. The actual banned characters, words and phrases are
operator configuration in `profile.json`, and a page transcribing them is stale the first time the owner adds one.
So this page carries the four kinds, and the command carries the list:

```bash
slopweaver slack voice-rules          # the shape, then every rule currently in force
```

That is the same trade the [verb list](../build/verbs) makes: the surface documented and the surface
installed cannot disagree, because only one of them is written down.
