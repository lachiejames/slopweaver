# Coverage and its bounds

What is actually in bronze, how far back it goes, and — the harder half — how you tell a gap from a
silence. A corpus that cannot say what it does **not** hold is a corpus you stop trusting the first time a
number surprises you.

<CoverageGates />

## The two Slack gates, and the one that was blamed for years of history it never touched

The phrase that started this was a real log line: `channel-membership cap 50 applied — <n> active
channel(s) not resolved`, with `<n>` in the hundreds. It reads like the thing keeping messages out of the corpus. It is not.

- **The channel-membership cap is OPT-IN and it bounds STRUCTURE metadata.** `--slack-membership-cap N`
  bounds how many channels get a `conversations.members` call during the structure lane — who belongs to
  a channel. Pass nothing and every active channel is resolved. It has never touched message ingestion.
- **A capped run stays honest about what it did not ask.** Every channel row carries a
  `membershipResolved` attribute, silver counts the `false` ones, and `derive` names them on its
  `structures:` line. Without it a capped channel and a genuinely empty one are both zero member edges
  downstream — measured at a cap of 50: hundreds of active channels never asked, and **zero** of the 50 asked came
  back with no members. Every zero in silver was an unasked question.
- **What gates message history is the backfill window**, so each conversation keeps its own cursor
  (`src/corpus/slack/channelWatermark.ts`) and archived channels are discovered too. One pooled cursor cannot
  express _never crawled_: a channel first seen after an earlier walk inherits a floor later than everything it
  holds. Measured under one: **over a hundred** reachable conversations carrying real messages held zero
  bronze rows, one of them a channel of thousands of messages reaching back more than a year.

<Callout type="warning">
  **`is_member: false` does not mean a channel cannot be read.** That belief comes from a join between captured content
  and the flag, never from asking Slack. Asked directly, the user token read **all but one of hundreds of** non-archived
  conversations, membership or not — the single refusal was a DM whose counterpart is deactivated. Membership is a fact
  about a channel, not the access boundary.
</Callout>

```bash
slopweaver refresh --source slack --coverage   # reports the remaining gap, and exits non-zero while any exists
```

## How far back each source reaches, and why they differ so much

A reading of one install, kept here for its **shape** rather than its numbers — every figure
below is a snapshot of one machine's disk on one day, and `refresh` moves all of them.

| Source | Dense history starts | Why it lands there                                                       |
| ------ | -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Slack  | ~3.8 months          | the backfill window; older pins and bookmarks are captured separately    |
| GitHub | ~3.7 months          | org-wide, no repo cap — stragglers are threads search re-discovers       |
| Notion | ~7.7 months          | bounded by the integration grant, not by anything in this repo           |
| Linear | **~2.8 years**       | nothing filters by team, project or age, and there is no membership gate |

**Linear is the outlier and the reason is structural**, not a setting somebody chose: its root list
queries are workspace-wide and `includeArchived` is on, so its history runs continuously back to 2023-12.
**Notion is the one source where "do we have all the history" is a question about a permission the owner
controls outside this codebase** — a Notion internal integration only sees what a human has explicitly
shared with it, and a widened grant backfills nothing until the next `refresh --source notion`.

## A refresh only appends, so bronze carries duplicates until it is compacted

A refresh never merges overlapping windows, and on that same reading **19% of what was on
disk was a duplicate capture** — concentrated in Linear and Notion, each of which had more than one
near-complete full-history window beside later incremental ones.

Nothing downstream is wrong because of it: `latestRecords` reads through the duplication at query time. It
is a disk and collation cost, and `compact` is what removes it — the `compact-corpus-daily` standing row, once
`slopweaver automation register-standing` has written it, declares a run due at 02:30 each night, so a
hand run is only for right now.

```bash
slopweaver dev manifest --out before.json       # snapshot the corpus first
slopweaver compact                              # merge overlapping windows, dedupe
slopweaver dev manifest --compare before.json   # prove it lost nothing it did not touch
```

## A clean miss is not the same as an empty corpus

A dogfood question asked about a real person **by a misspelled name** and got zero records back, which read
as a coverage gap. It was not one — the corpus held that person correctly, under the correct spelling,
across two sources, and re-asking returned three records out of twelve.

What actually happened: retrieval never consults `corpus/members/` to catch a near-miss spelling or offer a
"did you mean", so a name one character off the roster produces a **clean, silent miss** rather than a
match or a warning. That is a retrieval-reconciliation gap, and it is filed as one. The lesson generalises
past this install: a zero from a retrieval path is only evidence of absence if something proves the
question was asked the way the data was stored.
